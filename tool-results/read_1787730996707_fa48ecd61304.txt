Avoid these common mistakes to create dark themes that users will actually enjoy using.</p><p>Remember to use dark grays instead of pure black, soften text colors slightly, adjust color vibrancy, maintain elevation through lighter backgrounds, ensure interactive elements stand out, handle images thoughtfully, keep borders visible, maintain accessibility standards, test in real conditions, and keep your implementation simple.</p><p>Start with these principles and iterate based on user feedback. Your dark mode should feel intentional and comfortable, not like an afterthought.</p><hr><h2 id=\"heading-32-frequently-asked-questions\">Frequently Asked Questions</h2><h3 id=\"heading-33-should-i-use-gray-scale-or-custom-dark-colors\">Should I use gray-scale or custom dark colors?</h3><p>For most projects, Tailwind's built-in gray scale (<code>gray</code>, <code>slate</code>, <code>zinc</code>, or <code>neutral</code>) works perfectly. Use custom colors only if you have specific brand requirements. The built-in scales are carefully calibrated for readability and contrast.</p><h3 id=\"heading-34-how-do-i-test-if-my-colors-meet-accessibility-standards\">How do I test if my colors meet accessibility standards?</h3><p>Use browser DevTools or online tools like WebAIM's contrast checker. In Chrome DevTools, inspect an element and look for the contrast ratio indicator. Aim for at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text. Many design tools like Figma also include built-in contrast checkers.</p><h3 id=\"heading-35-do-i-need-different-images-for-dark-mode\">Do I need different images for dark mode?</h3><p>Not always, but it helps for logos, icons, and images with white backgrounds. For photos and general content images, reducing opacity slightly (<code>dark:opacity-90</code>) usually works well. Product images and screenshots might need custom dark versions if they contain a lot of white or light colors that clash with dark backgrounds.</p></div></div></div><div class=\"lg:hidden mb-12\"><div
